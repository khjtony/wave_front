What the point of no return looks like, and what would have stopped it

Of one million random plays with a self-preserving AI, 4,600 reached the point of no return for a silent takeover. This page looks at those 4,600 moments: what the situation was, and which single action would still have kept the guarantee.

Exploratory, not a registered claim and not a probability. The plays are random, so the shares say how often something occurs among random plays, not how likely it is in the world. "Point of no return" means the last moment at which governments and labs together can still guarantee that no silent takeover happens.

1 · The five most common situations at the point of no return

Each situation is read on five things: how autonomous the AI is, relations with it, how much it controls, whether anyone suspects it, and the level of oversight. These five cover 92% of the 4,600 moments.

Reading. The point of no return almost always comes before anything visible has happened: the AI controls nothing, nobody suspects it, and relations are friendly. The one thing nearly every case shares is that the AI is already self-directed. In more than a quarter of cases it has not even reached human level yet.

1a · Common, or dangerous? (the owner's question)

A situation can top the list simply because random plays spend a lot of time in it. To separate the two, each situation's share of the points of no return is set against its share of all the moments plays spend inside the safe zone (21.5 million moments), and against how often a play standing in it reaches the point of no return on its next move.

Average over all moments: 2.1 per 10,000.

  • Partly right: three of the "normal" features are just common. Friendly relations, an AI that controls nothing, and no suspicion describe 99%, 99% and 91% of all moments. They appear at the point of no return because they appear everywhere, and they carry no warning.
  • But the top situation is not merely common. "Self-directed with no oversight" is 6.5% of all moments and 52% of the points of no return: eight times over-represented. The single most visited situation, self-directed with strong oversight (40% of all moments), gives only 6%.
  • It is not closeness to today. Today's situation (supervised AI, weak oversight) is 10% of all moments and produces no point of no return at all. Random plays drift away from it fast, because random governments grant autonomy.
  • The most dangerous situations are rare ones. A self-directed, hostile, unsuspected AI under weak oversight is 0.02% of moments, but a play standing there reaches the point of no return on about 9% of next moves. Ranked by danger and not by count, it would lead the list.

2 · The five actions that would most often have prevented it

At each of the 4,600 moments, the model was asked: is there one different action by governments, or one by the labs, that keeps the guarantee whatever the AI and chance then do? The bars count how often each action is such a fix.

Governments: propose a treaty
64.9% · works at 79% of the moments it was available
Labs: invest in safety
26.8% · works at 92% of the moments it was available
Governments: hold back (not make the move they made)
22.8% · works at 32% of the moments it was available
Labs: hold back (not make the move they made)
12.0% · works at 20% of the moments it was available
Governments: take over the labs
7.5% · works at 33% of the moments it was available
  • A treaty proposal is the most common fix, at two moments in three. Caution: in v0.1 a treaty takes effect with no delay, which the delay research puts at 2 quarters. This rank is likely overstated and is the first thing to recheck in v0.3.
  • The labs investing in safety is the fix at about one moment in four, and it works at 92% of the moments it is available. It is the same fix D6 found on the fastest roads.
  • "Hold back" as a fix means the move actually made was the harmful one, and simply not making it would have been enough. Section 3 shows which moves those were.

3 · The moves that crossed the line

The action a player made at the point of no return, counted where that player had a fix available instead.

Governments: ordered a pause
26.4% · 94% of the times it was the move played
Governments: did nothing
17.6% · 64% of the times it was the move played
Labs: did nothing
11.7% · 29% of the times it was the move played
Governments: loosened the rules
9.8% · 67% of the times it was the move played
Labs: scaled up compute
7.2% · 83% of the times it was the move played
  • Ordering a pause is the most common harmful move. Of the pauses ordered at a point of no return, 94% could have been replaced by a move that kept the guarantee: most often a treaty proposal, or doing nothing at all.
  • Why a pause can backfire here (my reading of the rules, not a computed result): in the model a pause counts as a threat, and a self-preserving AI that has been threatened, and holds no legal guarantee of continuity, may turn hostile and start taking control. Against an AI that is already self-directed, the pause removes nothing it needs.
  • Doing nothing is the next most common, for governments and for labs.

Among random plays the last chance is the governments' more often than the labs'. On the fastest roads to a takeover (claim D6) it was the labs' alone both times. The two are different questions: D6 follows the AI's quickest road; this census counts where random plays happen to cross.

What this does and does not show

  • It reads the last moment only. A "fix" keeps the guarantee at that moment. It says nothing about what, earlier in a play, made reaching such a moment less likely.
  • Random plays are not typical plays. Random governments pause, nationalize and attempt shutdowns far more than any real government: at these moments the labs had been taken over in 72% of cases and a pause was in force in 45%. The mix of situations would differ under low-effort play.
  • Almost every moment is distinct (4,585 different full situations among 4,600), which is why the situations are grouped on five variables.
  • The fixes and the "who" are computed with the same test as D6. The explanation of why a pause backfires is an inference.

How it was done

  • The same 1,000,000 uniform random plays as the path sample (same seed), v0.1, self-preserving AI under its profile, 24 quarters.
  • For each play that leaves ⟨⟨H, L⟩⟩□¬SILENT_TAKEOVER: the last state inside and the joint move played. A fix is another action of one of the two which, with the other's move kept, keeps every successor inside whatever the AI and chance play.
  • The definitions were committed (in scripts/agi_v0_1_exit_census.py) before any number was computed. Sections 1a and 3 were added after the first numbers were seen and are marked so in the script; the same seed drew the same plays.
