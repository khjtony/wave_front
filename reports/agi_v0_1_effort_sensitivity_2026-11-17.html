How much do the effort-weighted results depend on the choices behind them?

Three checks on the effort-weighted census: how strongly players prefer cheap actions, which effort scores carry the result, and how the AI plays. The direction holds in all three. The sizes do not: they swing by a factor of ten or more.

Exploratory, not registered claims and not probabilities. v0.1, self-preserving AI, plays to 2040 Q4. "Point of no return" is for a silent takeover: governments and labs can no longer guarantee preventing it.

1 · Strength of the preference

Direction robust, size not

Any preference for cheap actions raises the bad endings. Between a weak and a standard preference the silent takeover goes from 0.3% to 3.3%.

2 · Which scores matter

Governments' treaty and deregulation costs

One point on either moves the point-of-no-return share between about 2% and 40%. The labs' scores, including the cost of racing, barely matter.

3 · How the AI plays

It decides the ending, not the point of no return

A lazy, random or determined AI gives 0.6%, 3.3% or 17% silent takeovers, while 17% to 22% of plays reach the point of no return in all three.

  • Two government scores dominate. If proposing a treaty is one point cheaper, the point-of-no-return share falls from 17% to under 2% and 95% of plays end contained; one point costlier and it doubles, with containment almost gone. Loosening the rules works the other way round: cheaper is much worse.
  • Pauses again look harmful. Making a pause costlier (so governments pause less) cuts the silent takeover from 3.2% to 0.9%. This matches the exit census, where a pause was the most common move that crossed the line.
  • The labs' scores barely matter, including the ones I was worried about: making racing, deploying or scaling cost the labs a point changes little. The result is not an artefact of "labs race for free".
  • So the weighted census is mostly a statement about how readily governments make treaties, in a model where a treaty takes effect at once. That is one more reason to add the treaty delay (v0.3) before leaning on these shares.

  • The point of no return hardly depends on the AI (17% to 22%). That is what the term means: it is set by what governments and labs do, against any AI.
  • What happens after it depends almost entirely on the AI. A determined AI turns 76% of those plays into a silent takeover; a random one 20%; a lazy one 3%.
  • This is why the point of no return is the more useful number. The takeover share needs an assumption about the AI's behaviour that nobody can supply. The point-of-no-return share does not.
  • The "determined" AI is a simple rule (take control if possible, else turn hostile, else escape supervision), not the best possible play. A better player would do at least as well.

What I take from this

  • Keep: the direction (the easy path is much more dangerous than random play), hostility as the gate, treaties and safety investment as the fixes, pauses as a common harmful move, and the point of no return as the number that does not depend on the AI.
  • Do not quote alone: any single share such as "3.3% silent takeovers". It moves tenfold with the width, with one treaty score, or with the AI's policy.
  • Next: the treaty delay (v0.3), since so much rides on treaties; then, if these censuses are to carry weight, register one on v0.2 with the width and the AI's policy fixed in advance.
